The staircase of abstractions

From the electromagnet to the programming paradigm, one step at a time. Each step answers what it is, why it exists, where it appears, and where you get stuck — and 5 of the passages you don't need to accept at face value: they build the piece right here on the screen.

The map begins almost empty on purpose. It only admits what someone read: nothing enters by plausibility, by consensus, nor because a model wrote it.

How to read. The map ascends: the electromagnet is the ground, the machine is the top — and the dotted golden line marks the place where the subject stops being electricity and becomes logic. The node's color indicates the regime; the key is right below it. A solid arrow has a verified citation against the book; a dashed arrow is something no one has read yet, and it is drawn because a map that hides what is missing lies about its own size.

Within each step, the four fields come with a stamp of origin: citation when the text is from the book, synthesis when it is a summary of a cited passage — and the passage opens beside it, so you can check if the summary is honest —, and craft · ratified when it is a judgment of the teacher, which no book writes — and each one brings the date when the author stood by it, because a field that changes after that date reverts to being a proposal. There is no stamp for “a model wrote”, and it is on purpose: see the last section.

electromagnet

physicslevel -1
what it iscitation
An iron bar that becomes an electromagnet while current flows through the wire wrapped around it.
the passage that supports
“If you take an iron bar, wrap it with a couple of hundred turns of thin insulated wire, and then run a current through the wire, the iron bar becomes a magnet.”Charles Petzold, Code: The Hidden Language of Computer Hardware and Software, 2ª ed. · cap. 7 — Telegraphs and Relays
why it existssynthesis
Because that's how electricity pulls something. Morse needed a signal that acted at a distance and couldn't use a lamp: the practical lamp wouldn't appear until 1879. Magnetism was left as the option.
the passage that supports
“Morse couldn’t use a lightbulb as his signaling device because a practical one wouldn’t be invented until 1879. Instead, Morse relied upon the phenomenon of electromagnetism.”Charles Petzold, Code: The Hidden Language of Computer Hardware and Software, 2ª ed. · cap. 7 — Telegraphs and Relays

relay

physicslevel 0
what it iscitation
An electromagnet that pulls a metal lever, and the lever is part of a switch that connects a battery to an output wire.
the passage that supports
“A relay is like a sounder in that an incoming current is used to power an electromagnet that pulls down a metal lever. The lever, however, is used as part of a switch connecting a battery to an outgoing wire.”Charles Petzold, Code: The Hidden Language of Computer Hardware and Software, 2ª ed. · cap. 7 — Telegraphs and Relays
why it existssynthesis
Because wire has resistance: the longer it is, the more it weakens the signal, and the telegraph line couldn't grow indefinitely. The relay listens to the weak signal that remains and uses a local battery to retransmit it strongly. It doesn't fix the wire—it restarts the signal.
the passage that supports
“the longer a length of wire becomes, the more resistance it has to the flow of electricity. This was a major impediment to long-distance telegraphy. … In this way, a weak incoming current is “amplified” to make a stronger outgoing current.”Charles Petzold, Code: The Hidden Language of Computer Hardware and Software, 2ª ed. · cap. 7 — Telegraphs and Relays
where it appearssynthesis
The start of your car, the contactor for the air conditioner, the control panel of a warehouse's automation. And, historically, the entire computer.
the passage that supports
“It’s a switch, surely, but a switch that’s turned on and off not by human hands but by an electrical current. You could do amazing things with such devices. You could actually assemble much of a computer with them.”Charles Petzold, Code: The Hidden Language of Computer Hardware and Software, 2ª ed. · cap. 7 — Telegraphs and Relays
where you get stuckcraft · ratified
The circuit that controls and the circuit controlled are two separate circuits that do not touch — only the magnetic field passes through. Those who merge the two into one cannot see why it's possible to chain relay to relay. And chaining is everything that comes after.

logic gate

combinationallevel 1
what it iscitation
The component where Boolean algebra meets electricity: it performs logical operation by blocking or allowing the current to pass.
the passage that supports
“Reduced to its essentials, a computer is a synthesis of Boolean algebra and electricity. The crucial components that embody this melding of math and hardware are known as logic gates. … Logic gates perform simple operations in Boolean logic by blocking or letting through the flow of electrical current.”Charles Petzold, Code: The Hidden Language of Computer Hardware and Software, 2ª ed. · cap. 8 — Relays and Gates
why it existssynthesis
Two relays in series only light the bulb if the two are activated — the E of Boolean, made of metal. And the advantage of the relay over the switch is that it is activated by another relay, not by a finger: that's it, and only that, which allows combining gate with gate until it becomes arithmetic.
the passage that supports
“Relays have an advantage over switches in that relays can be switched on and off by other relays rather than by fingers. This means that logic gates can be combined to perform more complex tasks, such as simple functions in arithmetic and, eventually, the workings of entire computers.”Charles Petzold, Code: The Hidden Language of Computer Hardware and Software, 2ª ed. · cap. 8 — Relays and Gates
where it appearscraft · ratified
Every condition of every program you've ever written. The if (a && b) of your last function is this gate, with sixty years of layers on top.

adder

combinationallevel 2
what it iscitation
Adds two bits and returns two: the sum bit and the carry bit.
the passage that supports
“adding a pair of binary numbers results in two bits, which are called the sum bit and the carry bit (as in “1 plus 1 equals 0, carry the 1”)”Charles Petzold, Code: The Hidden Language of Computer Hardware and Software, 2ª ed. · cap. 14 — Adding with Logic Gates
why it existssynthesis
Because adding a single column isn't enough. From the second column onward, there are three bits to add: the two from the column plus the carry-one that came from the previous one. That third input is what distinguishes the half-adder from the full adder.
the passage that supports
“We can use the half adder only for the addition of the rightmost column: 1 plus 1 equals 0, carry the 1. For the second column from the right, we really need to add three binary numbers because of the carry. And that goes for all subsequent columns.”Charles Petzold, Code: The Hidden Language of Computer Hardware and Software, 2ª ed. · cap. 14 — Adding with Logic Gates
where it appearscraft · ratified
Every sum of every processor in the world, including the one that summed the loop index you ran today. It's also where overflow lives: the carry-out that comes from the last column and has nowhere to go.

the passage that supports

But since we know what goes on inside the half adder, it’s more correctly termed a clear box.”Charles Petzold, Code: The Hidden Language of Computer Hardware and Software, 2ª ed. · cap. 14 — Adding with Logic Gates

level-triggered flip-flop

sequentiallevel 2
what it iscitation
The first circuit with two stable states for the same input. It stores information: remembers which switch was closed last.
the passage that supports
“We can say that this circuit has two stable states when both switches are open. Such a circuit is called a flip-flop… A flip-flop circuit retains information. It “remembers.” It only remembers what switch was most recently closed, but that is significant.”Charles Petzold, Code: The Hidden Language of Computer Hardware and Software, 2ª ed. · cap. 17 — Feedback and Flip-Flops
why it existscraft · ratified
Up to this step, the output depended only on the inputs of now. Without memory, there is no “before”: there is no accumulated count, no counting, no program. Everything the machine does over time begins here.

where it appearssynthesis
From 1918, by radio physicists Eccles and Jordan — before the computer. Today: every bit of every register, and the anti-jitter of every physical button you press.
the passage that supports
“The flip-flop dates from 1918 with the work of English radio physicists William Henry Eccles (1875–1966) and F.W. Jordan (1881–1941).”Charles Petzold, Code: The Hidden Language of Computer Hardware and Software, 2ª ed. · cap. 17 — Feedback and Flip-Flops
where you get stucksynthesis
The feedback. The output returns and becomes an input, so there is no first instant to start tracing the signal. Those who try to follow in chronological order enter a loop and conclude they didn't understand. The way to read is different: stop following the path and look for which combinations sustain themselves — the stable states.
the passage that supports
“The output of the NOR gate on the left is an input to the NOR gate on the right, and the output of that NOR gate is an input to the first NOR gate. This is a type of feedback. Indeed, just as in the oscillator, an output circles back to become an input.”Charles Petzold, Code: The Hidden Language of Computer Hardware and Software, 2ª ed. · cap. 17 — Feedback and Flip-Flops

The circuit that resembles

Press S, release. Press R, release. In both cases, the input returns to (0,0) — and the output is different. This is the definition of remembering, and it's what no single gate can do on its own.

edge-triggered flip-flop

sequentiallevel 3
what it iscitation
Store the data at the instant of transition of the clock, not during the entire time the clock is high. Internally, they are two level-triggered flip-flops in series.
the passage that supports
“An edge-triggered D-type flip-flop is constructed from two stages of level-triggered D-type flip-flops, wired together this way:”Charles Petzold, Code: The Hidden Language of Computer Hardware and Software, 2ª ed. · cap. 17 — Feedback and Flip-Flops
why it existscitation
Because the level leaks. While the clock is at 1, the data can change — and these changes pass through to the output. In a circuit that feeds back into its own output, this becomes an infinite loop.
the passage that supports
“This is what’s called an “infinite loop.” It occurs because the D-type flip-flop we designed was level-triggered. The Clock input must change its level from 0 to 1 in order for the value of the Data input to be stored in the latch. But during the time that the Clock input is 1, the Data input can change, and those changes will be reflected in the values of the outputs.”Charles Petzold, Code: The Hidden Language of Computer Hardware and Software, 2ª ed. · cap. 17 — Feedback and Flip-Flops
where it appearscraft · ratified
Every “clock” that is talked about in processor. The 3.6 GHz of your machine are 3.6 billion of these edges per second — not 3.6 billion instants in which the clock is on.

Level × edge, in the same clock

The two circuits, the same data, the same clock. The light band is the time when the clock is high: the level copies the data during the entire band — it leaks. The edge copies only on the dotted line. This is the passage where almost everyone gets stuck, and in prose the two sound the same.

architecturelevel 5
what it iscitation
Data paths — registers and operations — plus a controller that sequences the operations. Viewed this way, it's literally a wiring diagram.
the passage that supports
“To design a register machine, we must design its data paths (registers and operations) and the controller that sequences these operations. … If we view the arrows as wires and the X buttons as switches, the data-path diagram is very like the wiring diagram for a machine that could be constructed from electrical components.”Harold Abelson & Gerald Jay Sussman, Structure and Interpretation of Computer Programs, 2ª ed. · SICP §5.1 — Designing Register Machines

Because a loose piece does nothing in order. What was missing was a signal to send the pieces working together to execute an instruction stored in memory — and that's the signal that stitches the machine together.

compiler

languagelevel 7
what it iscitation
Translates a source program into an equivalent program — the object program — written in the machine's native language.
the passage that supports
“A compiler for a given source language and machine translates a source program into an equivalent program (called the object program) written in the machine's native language.”Harold Abelson & Gerald Jay Sussman, Structure and Interpretation of Computer Programs, 2ª ed. · SICP §5.5 — Compilation

Because interpreting is expensive: compared to interpretation, compilation gives a significant efficiency gain in execution. The price is the other side — the interpreter is better for development and debugging, because the source remains at hand during execution.

Why this way

A computation map generated by a model takes an afternoon and seems complete. The problem appears later: in the twin map of this house, the math-prerequisite-map, the entries were written by a local model and one of them claims, in the air until today, that two sets with the same elements can be different. It's false, and passed because no one read it.

This page has the same four fields as that map, and that's why the difference matters: here none of them came from a model. The ones that describe the piece came from Petzold himself, who is a professor and already answers “what it is” and “why it exists” in prose, with a chapter — the passage goes along with each field. The ones that say where the student gets stuck no book writes: they are the judgment of the teacher, enter marked as proposal and await ratification.

The new edges enter through a funnel: a local model proposes candidates, a gate checks the phrase character by character against the book, and what survives only becomes an edge after human judgment. In the first round: 48 proposed, 46 passed through the gate, 34 accepted. The model can find; it cannot assert.

And the 5 instruments exist because citation is warrant of second hand: it asks you to believe a book that you might not have in hand. The instrument constructs the piece in front of you. When the two agree, the arrow is supported by a published source and by something that moves — it is the highest standard that this house can achieve.
